# Writing style for the writeup

The writeup should read as though four graduate students wrote it. Machine-drafted
prose has a recognisable set of habits, and the checklist below is what we edit
against. Sources: Graham on writing like you talk, and the pattern lists that
circulate for spotting generated text.

## Method

Two passes, in this order.

1. **Read each sentence aloud.** If it is not how you would say it to a
   classmate who knows the project, rewrite it as what you would have said.
2. **Run the ban list below** over the result and fix what it catches.

Do not skip the first pass. The ban list catches surface tics; only the read
-aloud test catches prose that is grammatical, clean, and says nothing.

## Ban list

**Sentence shapes**

- Negation-antithesis: "it is not X, it is Y", "X isn't just Y", "not because
  X, but because Y". Say the positive claim on its own.
- Rule of three. Three parallel items in a row, especially adjectives, when two
  or four would be the honest count.
- Fragments used for drama. "Left uncorrected." "No single fix." Give every
  sentence a verb.
- Section-closing aphorisms. "A significant coefficient can measure the wrong
  construct entirely." State the finding and stop.
- Rhetorical questions as openers.
- False concession: "While X is impressive, Y remains a challenge."

**Punctuation and formatting**

- Em dashes. Use commas, semicolons, parentheses, or two sentences. Aim for
  zero in the writeup.
- Bold text mid-paragraph to mark a takeaway. Bold belongs in headings and
  table keys.
- Uniform paragraph length. Vary it deliberately.

**Vocabulary**

Replace on sight: delve, leverage, robust, comprehensive, crucial, pivotal,
underscore, showcase, highlight (as a verb for "show"), navigate (figurative),
landscape (figurative), realm, tapestry, testament to, seamless, meticulous,
nuanced, holistic, foster, harness, unlock, elevate, intricate, myriad,
plethora, encompass, transformative, cornerstone, paramount, ever-evolving.

Also: utilise (use), in order to (to), due to the fact that (because), serves as
(is), boasts (has).

**Transitions**

Moreover, Furthermore, Additionally, In conclusion, In summary, It is worth
noting that, Notably, When it comes to, That said. Restructure the sentence or
use "and", "but", "so".

**Content habits**

- Significance inflation. Do not call a result striking; give the number and
  let the reader judge.
- Hedge stacking: "could potentially", "may eventually". One hedge or none.
- Synonym cycling. Repeat the clearest word rather than varying for its own
  sake.
- Vague attribution. Name the source, the lecture, or the file.
- Low information density. Every paragraph should carry a fact, a number, or a
  turn in the argument.

## What to keep

Hedging that reflects real uncertainty is good academic writing, not a tic.
"Consistent with", "suggests", "we cannot separate these" are all fine when
they are true. The problem is hedging used as filler.

Long sentences with subordinate clauses are fine and normal in academic prose.
The tell is uniformity, not length.
